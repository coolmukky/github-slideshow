01

The Customer

Cedarline Health Group is a regional healthcare payer-provider. Roughly 8,000 employees across four hospitals and thirty-one clinics, plus a claims-processing business that handles the money. They have grown largely by acquisition — most recently swallowing a competitor two years ago, and with it an entire second data centre, absorbed as-is. They are mid-flight in a move to public cloud, and it has not been an orderly one.

What the business is trying to achieve

Answer the board.

After a security incident in March, the board asked what would have stopped it. Leadership still cannot answer, and the next review is coming.

Finish the cloud migration.

Claims analytics, the new patient portal and data science are all live in public cloud, and growing.

Protect patient and claims data without slowing clinical systems.

Regulated data; zero appetite for clinical downtime.

Do it with the team they have.

Hiring is slow, the security function is small, and knowledge is concentrated in very few heads.

Spend the window.

Budget is approved for this fiscal year and executive attention is live — both are explicitly temporary.

The problem they've asked you to solve

"We have budget, we have executive attention, and the two of those being true at the same time is rare and it won't last. What we don't have is a coherent plan — we have a pile of problems and a lot of opinions. Tell us where you'd start. And tell us why."

Note carefully. Cedarline has not given you a requirements list. They have given you a conversation. The problems are inside it — stated once, in passing, usually as a symptom rather than a cause. Nothing is numbered, highlighted or counted for you. Some of what they say is not a problem at all. Extracting the real pain points is the first thing you are scored on.

And every change is a small act of archaeology, because nobody will delete a rule — nobody can prove what it does. Our firewall environment has become too complex to manage manually. Open. Effectively open. Claims talks to clinical records, clinical records talks to imaging, imaging talks to the reporting warehouse — none of it controlled in any meaningful sense. It just grew. 03

How to Play

Object of the game. Turn an unstructured customer conversation into an architecture you would defend in front of the customer — and earn the right to the next meeting. You are not competing with the other tables. Every table can do well.

What you hand in

1
The Pain Board (Sheet 1)

Every real pain, in your words — and your WIDE/DEEP call at the top.

2
The Design Canvas (Section 05)

Platform, funded-pain number and reasoning written into the slots you touch.

3
The Grow & Defend card (Sheet 3)

Upsell, competitive alternatives, who runs it, and your reframes.

Locked at 36:00. Anything not written on them does not exist.

Take your seat

Your role card is already on the table. The proctor deals them. Don't negotiate — you'd be spending learning time on admin.

You will not be given the seat you live in. If your day job is firewalls, you are not the Network Security Engineer today. The stretch is the point.

Roles are lenses, not walls. Everyone contributes to every decision. Your seat tells you what you are responsible for making sure nobody misses.

The one decision that shapes everything

There are two honest answers to Priya, and you have to pick one. You cannot give her both in eleven minutes — and trying is how teams end up with six half-answers and nothing they would defend in front of her.

GO WIDEthe full picture
What you say to Priya
"Here is everything that's wrong in your environment, and what addresses each of it. Now let's talk about what to do first."
How you spend Round 3
Map every pain to a platform. ~2 minutes each. Named product, one line of reasoning, move on.
What it costs you
Two minutes buys a product name, not a justification. Priya can see you heard her — but not that you understood her business.
GO DEEPthe conviction
What you say to Priya
"Here are the three I would fund this year. Here is exactly why — and precisely why not the others."
How you spend Round 3
Solve three pains properly: platform, how it works, why it fits Cedarline, what it doesn't cover. Park the rest, with reasons.
What it costs you
You didn't put an answer against every problem she raised. If your reasons for parking are thin, it reads as an answer you didn't have — not a decision you made.

Neither is the safe answer. Both are scored fairly, and both can score well. What loses is not choosing — drifting into six shallow rows because nobody said out loud what the plan was. SEs make this call in real rooms, under roughly this much time pressure. Make it on purpose.

The four seats

mirrors Priya

IT Architect

Owns the shape of the answer, and the pen. Priya asked where to start. You are the one who has to answer her.

  1. Run the round. Force problems, not quoted sentences.
  2. Your round — drive the WIDE/DEEP call.
  3. Keep pace. Park anything that stalls.
  4. Check the upsell follows from what you funded.
  5. Deliver the readout. Lead with sequence.
mirrors Marcus

Network Security Engineer

Owns threat, detection, inspection, enforcement. If it's about seeing an attack or stopping one, it's yours. You carry the March incident.

  1. Name the detection and visibility problems.
  2. Argue the risk case.
  3. Your round — platforms, and what each does NOT catch.
  4. The gap you can't close is where the upsell lives.
  5. Pre-empt Marcus's objection.
mirrors Priya's team

Network Operations Engineer

Owns the estate on Monday morning. Consoles, rulebases, backlog — and the fact that two people hold all the knowledge. Your question: who actually operates this?

  1. Surface the operational pain.
  2. Veto anything undeployable this year.
  3. Own the policy plane. Fewer places to write policy, or more?
  4. Your round — services, partner, adoption support.
  5. Take "four new products for two people."
mirrors Dae-Sun & the app owners

Business Applications Team

Owns the applications — and the money. What does this cost the business today, and what does the board hear?

  1. Keep the cloud and app voice from being drowned out.
  2. Your round — every funded pain needs a business reason.
  3. Ask what breaks. Segmentation that downs claims is not a win.
  4. Their alternatives to Cisco, and why we win.
  5. Take budget and business impact.

Nobody plays the customer. All four of you are on the Cisco side. In Round 5 your table proctor plays Cedarline. Which is why each seat has a counterpart: before they speak, ask what your counterpart would object to — and answer it first. Anticipating an objection scores higher than surviving one.

ROUND 0Read5 min · 0:00–5:00
Silent. No discussion. Read the transcript and mark anything that sounds like a problem.

Your role card is already in front of you.

By the end: everyone has read it properly, and nobody has said a product name out loud.

ROUND 1Diagnose7 min · 5:00–12:00
Name every pain point, in your own words — the problem underneath the sentence, not the sentence. Then strike out anything that isn't one.

House rule: no products this round.

Check: has every voice in the room been heard? Did you list something the customer told you to park?

ROUND 2Call Your Play3 min · 12:00–15:00
Say it out loud and write it at the top of Sheet 1: WIDE or DEEP.

If DEEP, name the three pains you're funding and one sentence each on why — plus one sentence on what you tell Priya about the rest. If WIDE, rank the pains, because you'll run out of clock before you run out of rows.

The most common way to lose is to never make this call.

ROUND 3Design11 min · 15:00–26:00
Eleven minutes on the Design Canvas. For every slot you touch: platform (named), which pain it closes, how it works, and why it fits Cedarline.

WIDE ≈ 2 min a pain. DEEP ≈ 3½.

The bar, in either play: if your reasoning would read the same for any customer, it isn't reasoning — it's a brochure.

ROUND 4Grow6 min · 26:00–32:00
Three things, two minutes each.

(a) The upsell — what your board doesn't cover, the platform that closes it, and the line in the transcript that points there. (b) Cedarline's alternatives to Cisco, and why yours wins for a team this size. (c) Who deploys and operates this.

(b) and (c) take ninety seconds each and teams leave them blank every time. They are also the two questions a customer asks the moment your design lands.

ROUND 5Defend & Lock4 min · 32:00–36:00
Around the table once, a minute each. Each seat names the objection its counterpart would raise — and the reframe. Write both down.

A reframe that only happened out loud didn't happen. At 36:00 the board is locked.

★ READOUTScoring4 min · 36:00–40:00
Sixty seconds, standing at the board: the pains you took on and why, the architecture, the upsell.

Then the proctor — playing Cedarline — asks one question. Usually: "You left problems on the table. What do you want me to do about them?"

The Design Canvas

This is Cedarline's environment drawn as a Hybrid Mesh Firewall board — campus and branch, internet and SaaS, two data centres, three public clouds, and a policy plane running underneath all of it. The nine numbered slots are the places policy could be enforced. None of them are filled in. That is your job — and in Round 5 you will stand at this board and explain the architecture from it.

The board is your design. There is no separate solution table. You write the platform, the pain number and the reasoning straight into the slots. You will fill three or four slots. Not nine. A board with nine half-answers loses to a board with four defended ones. An empty slot you can explain is a design decision. An empty slot you can't is a gap.

SLOT 1

Campus & clinic edge

Hospitals, clinics, billing workstations, remote clinical staff.

SLOT 2

Internet & SaaS edge

Outbound and the encrypted-traffic blind spot.

SLOT 3

DC-1 perimeter (owned)

Claims, clinical records, imaging, warehouse.

SLOT 4

DC-1 east-west

The flat, effectively-open interior.

SLOT 5

DC-2 perimeter (acquired)

Inherited rulebase nobody can prove.

SLOT 6

AWS — claims analytics

Native security groups, one team.

SLOT 7

Azure — patient portal

NSGs, a different team, a different model.

SLOT 8

GCP — data science

Stood up independently, found out afterwards.

SLOT 9

The policy plane

Where policy is written once and pushed everywhere. Not optional.

Slot 9 is the one teams forget. A board full of enforcement points with no policy plane is not an architecture — it's a shopping list, and it makes Priya's problem worse, not better. She has three consoles today. If your answer hands her more places to write policy rather than fewer, you have not listened to her.

For every slot you touch, answer these

  1. Does Cedarline need enforcement here at all? A slot left empty on purpose, and defended, scores. A silent gap does not.
  2. Which platform goes here — named, not a category? And which pain from Sheet 1 does it close? Write the number in the slot.
  3. Does this survive their constraints? Compliance killed decryption twice. Segmentation died three times on ten thousand hand-written rules. Two people run the whole estate.
  4. Trace one policy across the board. "Billing users may not reach the imaging store." Where is it written? Where is it enforced? How many places did you have to touch?
  5. What does the finished board NOT cover? That is where your upsell lives. Go and find it.

Half of this is won after the architecture is finished. Three of the six things you're measured on — what else could they buy, how you handle pushback, who runs it once it's live — have nothing to do with your design. They're decided entirely in Rounds 4 and 5, and they are the same work whichever play you called. That isn't an artefact of the scoring. It's the job.

A

Written beats spoken

You are scored on the board plus the 60-second readout. A brilliant point made at minute 20 and never captured does not exist at 36:00.

B

An unfinished row is worth nothing

Coverage only counts where the mapping is accurate. Six product names with no reasoning isn't a wide play — it's a blank sheet with logos on it.

What Good Looks Like

This example is from a different customer, in a different technology domain — deliberately. It shows you the standard expected in a slot and on the Grow card. It tells you nothing about Cedarline, and none of it belongs on your board.

Example customer: Harborline Freight. Mid-size logistics operator. In their discovery call the Operations Manager said: "Every shift, the handheld scanners in the north warehouse drop off somewhere around aisle 40, and the crew just gives up and writes the manifest on paper. We find out at the end of the day, when the counts don't reconcile."

The Pain Board entry

Weak — repeats the quote

"Scanners drop off around aisle 40."

Strong — names the problem underneath

Wireless coverage gaps in the north warehouse are causing an undetected data-integrity failure. Staff fall back to paper; the discrepancy surfaces a full shift late. The pain is the reconciliation loss, not the dead zone. Fund it — the only pain with a measurable financial loss, and it compounds every shift it isn't fixed.

The same slot on the canvas — filled badly, then well

Slot · warehouse access edge
Platform

Something for wireless coverage. · Pain #1

Why it fits

It would fix the Wi-Fi and give them better coverage, which improves productivity.

Slot · warehouse access edge
Platform

Cisco Meraki MR-series access points, managed from the Meraki dashboard. · Pain #1

How it works

Re-survey and re-site the APs to close the aisle-40 gap; the dashboard's client-health and roaming telemetry surfaces drop-offs as they happen.

Why it fits Harborline

No dedicated network staff — a cloud-managed dashboard means the warehouse supervisor sees a coverage problem without raising a ticket. And it attacks the real pain: they stop finding out a shift late.

What it doesn't solve

If the scanners themselves are failing over badly, coverage alone won't fix it — client behaviour needs checking too.

The platform names a product

The weak slot names a category. The strong slot names a product.

The justification is specific

The weak one would be true for any customer on earth. The strong one turns on what this customer said: no network staff; the loss is discovered a shift late.

It says what it doesn't cover

That is not a weakness — it's the difference between an engineer and a brochure.

The Grow & Defend card, done well

PromptAnswer
The upsellCisco Spaces — location analytics riding on the same wireless estate. The gap: Harborline knows THAT the counts don't reconcile; they never know WHERE the process broke. The line that points here: "we find out at the end of the day" — a visibility complaint, the one they didn't ask us to fix. Why now: rides the same hardware refresh, no second deployment. Depends on: the AP re-siting landing first.
Their alternatives to usRe-site their existing APs and change nothing else — cheapest, fixes the dead zone but not the day-late discovery. Or go to their incumbent vendor, who sells them a controller they'd then have to run. We win because they have no network staff: every alternative solves coverage and hands them an operational burden they cannot carry.
Who runs itPartner-led site survey and install; the warehouse supervisor owns the dashboard day to day — no new headcount, which is the only version of this Harborline can actually sustain.

The trap this example exists to show you. That slot is technically excellent — and if the team had stopped there, they'd have lost half their marks. The two rows under the upsell — what else could they buy, and who runs it — took ninety seconds each and are worth as much as the entire design. That is the single most common way good SEs lose in this room, and in the real one.
